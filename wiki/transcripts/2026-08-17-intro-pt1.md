## Why we record; the goal · 00:00

A recording made while everyone is online is first class: the recorder knows who said what — unlike in a shared room — the transcript can be downloaded as subtitles, and handed to any agent, which will then know a lot. (Jona's research suggests Microsoft Copilot also takes the shared screen into account.)

The personal introductions already happened, unrecorded, and Jona found them really valuable. Recording them would have given a foundation, because we are the people who will be working on this — but the personal comes in anyway, as we care and as we work, so nothing is lost.

The goal: a knowledge structure around the project that is more *uniform* than today. How the work will go is still unknown. First class for an agent means the agent can reach everything, and everything is reachable in the same good way. That is the whole aim.

AI cannot do all of it. What humans bring is gut feeling: the deeper Jona gets into a subject, the less he reads of the big things the model writes and the more he steers by feel — the model does most of the magic, the human steers a little. Everything about AI, and about how the knowledge is written, goes into the knowledge itself; the work is very meta, and meta perspectives are welcome. It is built together: everyone contributes questions — "this is not clear", "why is that?", "are you sure about that section?" — with no obligation to do much, but everyone touches it and cares for it. The people aspects come in through that care.

## Jona and the history of the project · 02:56

Jona is the standing maintainer: in the project since day one, leading the technical side.

The first half year he was on the bench — IC, "intermission consultant", the Alten term for being between assignments — right after over a year with a social NGO, and this social project arrived at the same time: a great coincidence. He spent that half year developing with the first two interns from the same school (four months, directly), plus a few short stints by other bench consultants. Those months built a strong foundation.

The method then was gut feeling rather than a traditional UX or documentation process — but properly thought through, properly researched, given time, with a three-week incubation to test everything and attack it from different angles once it seemed right. AI at the time was chat: useful for research, questions and code in many ways, but not integrated. Today Jona sets up matrices of agents to attack a problem from different angles automatically; then, the attack was human, with AI as a tool.

Since then development has taken a really long time: Jona's availability became quite limited, and being a mentor was itself something to learn — he burnt out a little at the start of it; a really interesting learning experience. So the project is interestingly old, because development took its time. That is also part of its freedom: there are no obligations — on the interns either, beyond office hours to be discussed.

Probably in many companies you will find this: entering code that has been there ten years or more, and keeping it useful or upgrading it. It is very common for things to fall out of care and back into care. Write-deploy-forget could happen in some cases, but Jona doesn't think it uncommon that every product needs continuous improvement.

#### Fold: Jona's background, and a drag-and-drop library · 12:09

Jona is self-taught — no formal education, coding since age ten. His first proper job, where he stayed a few years, came before kids, with a strong passion, and he went into a black hole of really fun stuff. For the first half year the company didn't know what to trust a junior with, so they tested him: build a drag-and-drop library.

Drag and drop is something native programs are good at — the operating system had better be, or you hate it. On the web it still hasn't transcended. Technology is compromise everywhere, and drag and drop is the web's compromise made visible; a lesson in not trusting things. Did the people behind the web know what it would become? Some predicted it, but couldn't steer the sheep.

##### Fold: the history of the web · 13:46

The web began as documents. No server side versus client side, no JavaScript: the browser requested a file and showed it — but the file could link to another. A file system on steroids, files linking files, some of them images. Genuinely high-tech, still not perfected by the human race. Then styling, then JavaScript, then the era of single-page applications.

Here Jona names what he is doing:

> I'm not going to stop myself from speaking on some planes of detail now, because it's sharing my passion, but it's also sharing useful things, and we're getting it into this recording. I use the terminology *fold* a lot. When I'm working with agents or with knowledge, we're doing folds. So now I divert and talk about something else — that is a fold. There's something in that fold that is valuable to care for.

The server-side-first era gave way to what Angular opened: put a hash after the domain and anything can follow — it still points to `index.html`, so the URL changes without a reload, and unlike query params the hash can be caught in JavaScript. That made the first JavaScript router possible: a virtual space where the browser stops doing what it used to do — links caught, virtual entries on the history stack. Angular did that (from Google or acquired by Google, Jona isn't sure); React came next, from Facebook — acquired or seeded, likewise unsure.

What followed was building, in the browser, what usually lives outside it: proper applications. Facebook is one — it even has games. That is a curse with mixed emotions attached: VS Code and Teams are web, a Chromium instance running as an OS application; someone on tech social media complained that calendar plus Teams plus one more thing consumed all sixteen gigabytes of RAM. It is a compromise — but there is something the browser has that native lacks. People build there because it saves time: one unified, first-class place, no multiple teams on separate platforms to keep in sync.

##### Back: react-beautiful-dnd and Redux · 17:38

One fold back: React, and the drag-and-drop task. The big name on the block was *react-beautiful-dnd* — from a developer at PayPal, per Jona [Ed: written by Alex Reardon at Atlassian] — which did lists. It could not drag from a list and drop onto a grid the way an operating system can; it hard-coded one thing as drag and drop and shipped it, and it became huge because everyone wanted it — everyone is doing really cool stuff on the web, and you can't drag and drop it. Build drag and drop yourself and you meet every compromise the browser is — performance first: lift ten items out of a DOM tree with CSS so they are gone yet look right at sixty frames per second, drag them, hover them over a grid whose items shuffle and animate, drop. The DOM tree is not made for that.

Jona built it in React with Redux — a name the interns may not have met; they've done Vue at school, if they've started with Vue, not React. React itself takes from knowledge people have had since the dawn of computers — how you do things well; part of it is founded in lore and ancient know-how. Redux was married to React in the beginning: nobody built React without it. Redux, too, comes from functional roots — clear principles, called the Flux architecture, though that name is more modern than the ideas.

The realisation that came out of it: when working with a technology, know *why* it is big. People spent years building it. How do they want it used? Was that ever documented properly? No — just as this project's documentation isn't — but there are conferences, people talking, and Jona wanted all of it, and got sold.

### How to approach code you don't understand · 22:45

"Dropped in the jungle" has been the interns' analogy for arriving here; Jona didn't always have time, and people were left to their own devices for a while. So: how to approach code you don't understand — because we are still trying new technologies, and because even in a known technology, opening a project brings "what the ****?"

Start by finding a safe space. This is psychoanalytical, and so is code: "right now I'm not anxious, because I understand everything in this file" — that file is the safe space. The next file won't be. Then think like a compiler: a variable is established here, used there — copy its value into place in your head. Reading code is simulating being a compiler.

Then functions. Functions are folds; the technical term is a closure — a space where memory references behave differently, where what is established inside doesn't exist outside. Files have closures too. Closures in code and folds in knowledge behave alike: this references a function, go there, it relies on another, go there. Start somewhere, move a little, gain comprehension. If it's too much, be honest: I can't run ahead like that.

AI is the gateway drug: paste it in and expect what you need. That makes it easy to run ahead of yourself. When a little comprehension arrives, that is great; if a run needed five jumps to understand, go back, try something else, or use AI. Moving forward that way is how one becomes a good developer.

It holds for prose too. Good prose folds knowledge. Jona has his own principles for AI on how to write prose, how to write knowledge, how to fold. Don't understand a paragraph? Great — now there is something you *know* you don't understand, and it has keywords. Do I understand this sentence? It is how one reads at school; the same process.

### The three technical READMEs as gates · 27:28

Jona prioritised technical documentation. The monorepo has three modules, the pillars of the web app: front end, back end, and infrastructure — infrastructure as code, which the interns' school does not teach (asked, they said no). Good knowledge structure, by contrast, is still not understood: people use markdown, manage, do great work, but as a race we are still exploring. Infrastructure as code has a head start — it became popular with Docker, some fifteen years ago.

#### Fold: infrastructure as code, through history · 28:49

##### Java · 28:49

A fold into Java, because it matters. Shudong was the first senior Java developer Jona really got to spend time and talk with, and the teachings carried a feeling as much as facts — like grandma telling history; a bit of a revolutionary somewhere, big respect.

On Hacker News and tech socials, people say Java is ****, and they have a point — everyone understands some things and not others. But Java did something really cool. Before it, you were closer to the metal. C was revolutionary — Dennis Ritchie was — so much better than what came before, and writing an operating system, Unix, in C was very cool; but you still compile for the architecture you are on. Java runs your program inside a program that is itself compiled for the architecture: the Java runtime. Compile the Java file, and it just works. People love that one.

It did more: containerization, before Docker — asked whether they had learned anything about Docker yet, the interns said not really — the Java runtime, and web-related runtimes on top of it. Details later (containers and servlets are where Shudong will be great); the point is Java did both containerization and cross-platform hard, and both matter.

##### Git · 31:28

Whether Git was a thing when Java arrived, Jona wasn't sure — he thinks Java came before Git got popular, so perhaps they came at the same time; that would be interesting to know. [Ed: Java 1995; Git 2005.] Git revolutionised source code. Before it, perhaps a shared FTP server — imagine working that way. Git is code as code, code as commits, code in a database — an atomic database with principles from functional programming, an interesting study that Jona loves. One source of truth, the remote; one way to merge; a structure that understands code, diffs it, tracks change over time. Fantasise about a team on an FTP server versus Git.

##### The onion, and drift · 32:30

An application is an onion — the picture Jona used a year ago to explain infrastructure as code to a client. At the centre is the code: pure, a diamond, under Git, fully controlled. Around it, layer on layer: the Java containerization, the runtime, maybe Docker, the server hosting Docker, whatever manages many servers, the hardware that creates the virtual servers in the first place. Git speaks for a tiny speck of what an application is.

Infrastructure as code does to every other layer what Git did to the centre. Set up Hjulverkstan on a new Linux server by hand — from a Mac, perhaps — user, permissions, Java, pull the back end and the front end, type commands until it runs: what remains is a shell history, as an FTP server might have one, and nothing more. Every command mutated something, with no rollback as in Git — unless a snapshot happened to exist and the technology to restore it. That is not infrastructure as code. That is drift. A client's application that has run for ten years makes it concrete: log into that server and you meet ten thousand commands by twenty users — the FTP story of source code, again. Infrastructure as code takes all those layers and lets them be programmed in files tracked in Git, so every layer can be in Git. For Hjulverkstan that is CDK — infrastructure as code against AWS — alongside `api`, the back end, and `web`.

## The gap: what flows from above · 36:29

The landscape has its bottom-up things; there also has to be something that invites and takes you in. Purpose, some kind of context. Without obligation, nobody may even care to go bottom-up — and this is an open-source repository, so it should be portrayed so that people *want* to go in; for the interns too, things should be found rather than done out of obligation. Some combination of the two.

This is Jona's freshest painting, from this week, and the nut is not cracked: what is it that flows from above, down to the bottom-up? Goals are a big part; understanding goals is really important. That is the gap.

The gates at the bottom exist: how a developer works here is defined. A senior developer will have no questions — might be angry, might disagree, but no questions. A newcomer will at least know, as with the guidelines, which paragraph or section the questions attach to. It is structured. The gate around the code does not need doing; what is missing is above it.

## Thesis: the code is not the important artifact · 37:30

Why the gates exist at all is interesting. Speaking for both, Jona and Shudong see code as legacy — no longer that important, and in their experience not what the field values either. Hiring — so far someone, maybe soon something — turns on the personal: communication, being a being that is exposed and wants to collaborate. The technical, the code, less so.

So, with AI and the knowledge structure to be built: it ought to be so well defined that the front end could be removed as code and regenerated with AI in one shot. That is the definition of a complete knowledge structure — the code can be produced without touching it. There is a balance, and getting there is a very big leap. Process is not being set by Jona and no document will be handed down; how to approach it is to be talked out together.

The gates that exist are helpful: the guidelines — a core gate, a philosophical gate, a values gate — and the technical ones that stem from it. They will probably be purified; parts can get much better. The front-end one is a tough read, nothing like a knowledge structure that invites you in and lets you fold. The back-end one says how we build, how details are handled, how validation is done — after reading it one should be able to develop with confidence and excellence, by the established rules and laws.
