## 1. The partnership · 00:33

Alten has taken more than ten interns into Hjulverkstan, in a project about two and a half years old. Interns come for a first period of ten weeks in the autumn and a second of about four months in the spring, and this autumn's are the fourth round. Consultants between assignments have worked on it too, more at the start than now.

The partnership was not really in place at the start. Alten has two kinds of manager: business managers, responsible for staff, and managers who handle budgets and projects in operation. Frida, a business manager who had travelled and worked with Save the Children before, had a connection to Susanne, and that is how it began. Alten has a few hundred people in Gothenburg, most of them out at clients, and those in the office met Save the Children.

Alten is a large company, some fifty thousand employees across the world, very top-down, and good at what it does, with a clear template for how it works, thinks and counts. Working for free does not fit that template. Hjulverkstan came from Frida, not from management, which made it hard. Alten then went through changes, Frida moved on, and a new management for Sweden has been in place for a year. Over that year the partnership has been on the table: what to do next, and a properly written contract between the two, with its ambition. Much happened in the spring, but since the summer no one has taken it up, and it is on ice.

## 2. Why Alten works this way · 07:00

Jona went deep into how AI works, and the more he learned, the more he felt that the capability available has advanced very fast, while we are far behind in putting it into practice in how we work.

The short history, as he tells it, begins before computers existed, when people drew how they would work, and it was very philosophical. In the seventies came the first operating systems. They were complicated, and to build a program you built walls around it. There was a real revolutionary spirit then, before the race between Mac and Windows, and Dennis Ritchie drew the first operating system that did not build walls. Unix, and Linux after it, on which the planet's infrastructure is built, rest on small, simple building blocks, like Lego: the same at the foundation and for everyone who builds on the computer, so that everything works together and builds on everything else. That is why hackers love Linux, and it resonates with Hjulverkstan's values: it is of the people, organic, transparent and simple, and whatever one person builds makes it easier for others to build on it.

Then Mac and Windows grew, and market, economics and politics came into it. Still, in the first ten years of the 2000s, we largely had our own files. One of Alten's clients helps companies keep up with changes in the law. Before it had an application, it sent letters and binders of ready material, almost like a self-help book, a place where someone owns the whole bundle; then zip files of Word documents laid out in a structure. Files were owned in a different way: a file is whole, it can be held and moved, and a company of a few hundred people can share a server where everyone owns and finds things together.

Then came the cloud. Applications and the web grew capable enough to live on the web, and the last piece was lost. Programs had already become walls again while we kept the files; now the files are mostly lost too. AI has arrived as a kind of upgrade, but our information lies in separate locked rooms, and the AI we chat with has, like us, no access to all of it. So we are not integrated with this evolution, however fast it is.

Jona's vision is computers as they were seen in the seventies, now with AI: a medium that joins us, so that all our understanding and material hangs together, easy to reach and to understand, with AI to help. We do not have that now. It is the big gap in the market, and many fill it by building further in the environment of walls and locked rooms.

## 3. What the pilot builds, and what it does not · 13:16

The vision is large, so the pilot built this autumn is a compromise, kept very simple, yet it already gives a great deal.

It builds no new AI. It uses what every programmer uses, and some who do not program use too: an AI such as Claude from Anthropic or Codex from OpenAI, run not on the web but on your own computer. A session is connected to one folder and has access to that folder only. It can read and write every file there, a first-class participant just like you, so everything in the folder is gathered and available, not in locked rooms. The cost of the compromise is that the AI is for the developers: not everyone can work with AI in the same medium yet.

Nor does it build a new place for the knowledge, such as a new kind of database where everyone keeps their files and information, since that too is a large undertaking. Instead it uses what developers have used for the last fifteen years: keeping track of files, changing them, and keeping every version over time. Every application is really a folder of code. A change is sent up and everyone sees it, and everything is kept in a long history. AI is very good at looking back through that history and understanding it, and with that safety nothing can be lost.

What the pilot does instead is ask what knowledge is and how it is built well, and begin building the knowledge that way. Some think AI is great and others a catastrophe, and many expect it to manage everything at once, which it does not. What AI needs is what a person needs who is new to everything, or junior. To use AI to keep building the Hjulverkstan portal or the website, or further, Returhuset, without doing it by hand, it needs what a relatively junior developer needs: the history Jona tells, what programming is, how good things are built, how people collaborate, and how documentation and knowledge are structured. Then the background on the client, which matters for Hjulverkstan and Save the Children, where much lies between the lines. Then the requirements broken down from it, what has been built, how it works, and the decisions behind it.

Those pieces together give a complete ground. Where the ground has an obvious hole, AI will hallucinate in the gap, without knowing it does, and without us knowing either, just as someone junior and new to the project might. So the ground matters, and so does the study of how to build it. It is built in folders of text files in markdown, which programmers use. A Word file is proprietary, walled in, and cannot simply be opened in a text editor to see what it says; markdown opens in anything and can be changed in anything.

### 4.1 What the rule is · 22:06

Jona calls it process philosophy, and much of it ontology. Prose is always linear: as when we talk, you have to start somewhere and go somewhere, and you need to know a good deal to talk about anything. Some academic books, on electronics for instance, go bottom-up through a great deal of detail and only in the last chapter give what feels good. Going the other way is hard, since then you keep reading things that feel bigger than yourself. The force the rule works with is that you cannot run ahead of the reader, yet you want the most important first. So it gives the biggest wins it can without running ahead of the reader.

Readers differ: programmers junior and senior, a new client, a manager. But they share a picture at the start, some common goal, and that is the most important win, since nothing can matter more than what is shared. From there it breaks down into smaller and smaller wins and more detail, and every part begins by saying what reading it gives. It is presentation technique: the headline first, and then you choose how far down to go.

For one of Jona's clients this changes the economics. Before, you paid millions to have an application built, and then it was fixed, and changing or developing it cost proportionally as much. With the ground laid, AI and people work in a different way: all further development is authorship, building new ideas grounded in what is already there. Everything compounds, each new piece of writing gets easier, and the code can be generated from it. Code has been rather expensive; that changes. Everyone talks about AI, but this is more grounded, practical and simple, and for building something not yet finished it feels exactly right.

It suits working with interns too. It is a platform to learn from, where the next person can hear all this about Unix and the background at their own pace. And it lets both the interns, new as they are, and those who know more use AI to take the last part of Hjulverkstan home.

## 5. What it can be for Save the Children · 26:16

The work is about much more than the code. Growing something organically, never running ahead of yourself, with everything resting on the knowledge between people, is exactly what Save the Children does in Gothenburg, so Jona's vision here grows wider too.

Five weeks ago they talked about what knowledge interests the people in the workshops: understanding how the portal works, but also repairs and what is known about them. Collecting knowledge rests on the same cornerstones whether it is for the repairers, for Save the Children or for Alten. Some areas will be more for one than for the others, but they are still valuable to all, and the idea of the rule is that everything compounds as more knowledge comes in.

Making it the knowledge structure Save the Children works in is a high bar, many steps ahead. But the pilot has a website, the wiki published through GitHub Pages, not yet connected to an address of its own, such as wiki.hjulverkstan.org [?], and with little on it so far. Since Alten works on Hjulverkstan pro bono and everything is open source, with no secret code and nothing hidden, the knowledge can be open. What is learned from Daniel goes in, so knowledge about how repairs are done can be written, and the people in the workshops can use it; and it is the same knowledge Alten needs to understand the applications and build on them. So the repairers are an obvious first meeting point. So is Save the Children's own side: what the portal and the website are, how the tools are used and the website is changed, as ground for whoever updates it and for development from there. It would reasonably not reach far into Save the Children's own work, but it can include the part where Save the Children meets Alten.

For now it is read-only. Collaborating in it would need new AI and a new data store, which the pilot does not build. Still, much can be understood just by meeting like this, recording, and letting AI put it in. For another client, Jona spent perhaps three minutes on an interview that raised new questions; the AI took the file and updated every place the new perspective changed, and did it remarkably well.

So meetings are a good way, step by step: as Save the Children has needs and things to present, they meet and talk, and it comes in. A recorded and transcribed conversation is better than a finished text written to be posted. The AI takes in the whole conversation and distributes the relevant parts into the texts, which grow more complete. A small next step could be a way to leave comments, without collaborating in the text itself, and Alten will draw up a proposal. That would help a great deal: Save the Children could correct what they find wrong, rather than quoting back and forth in emails.

Jona's own interest goes further. Having seen Returhuset, he wants to build what he really wants to build, the new medium, and he does not know how much room Alten has for it, since Alten builds applications for people, not new technology. So they take it a step at a time. The knowledge base is not built up much yet, but whatever the future holds, it has a practical place now.

## 6. The portal is ready · 33:07

Save the Children worried that the knowledge base would take long to build, and asked how the admin part can be used today. They plan to make user guides for it, and thought all the repairers would use admin. On publishing, little remains: each time changes are to be published, Alten has to do it, but Save the Children can still build and translate material, so it is not a blocker. The answer to the rest sorted out the portal's parts.

The portal has three parts. *Shop* is logged in on one computer per workshop: booked bikes and repairs. *Admin* is used rarely, to reset a password, add users or staff, or add a new location, and reasonably by one responsible person, such as Daniel in Frölunda. *Web edit* changes everything on the website, in several languages, blog posts with images and text included, and it too is likely run by about one person. Material in the portal, such as photographing the bikes for rent, is made by one or a few decided people, not by the many young people and repairers; how often, and in what frame, is Save the Children's to plan.

Shop was kicked off in the spring, with staff from the different workshops gathered in Frölunda, but it never took off. The kickoff gave about five points of feedback, among them being more in line with GDPR and changes Daniel asked for. All of it is fixed, and the portal is ready for production.

The plan is to run through it once more, then use it for real in the workshops, with real customers and bikes to rent out. The first week could be spent playing in the test environment, to land before taking real customers, and the workshops could reasonably start one at a time. The low season helps: there is time to work with it, and to make the material.

The portal goes before the knowledge base, since it is simple: in the spring, staff understood it in about an hour, tried it and saw no problems. So the workshops start on it from the event on 24 October, with trial periods, and a person more responsible at each site who keeps an overview, so that people roll up their sleeves and use it. There is a learning phase, and the knowledge base comes later.
